## How the Game Plays — and Why It's Built for Speedrunning

The premise is literal. You search a haystack for a needle. There is no meter telling you how close you are, no map marker, and no way to brute-force the answer except by looking. Runs are short, retries are cheap, and the skill ceiling is set almost entirely by how well you can systematize looking.

That combination — short run length, low reset cost, high repeatability — is the classic recipe for a competitive leaderboard. When a full attempt takes only a few minutes, players will attempt it hundreds of times, and the community's collective knowledge compounds quickly.

| Design trait | Effect on a run | Why it matters for records |
|---|---|---|
| Single objective | No route branching or side goals | Everyone competes on the same task |
| Short attempt length | Resets are cheap and fast | High attempt volume, faster improvement |

Most leaderboards for a game like this separate submissions into a few familiar buckets:

| Category | What it usually means | Typical verification |

Two rules trip up almost everyone:

- **Timing method.** Know whether the board times from the first input, from a menu confirmation, or from the moment control is granted. A run that looks fast can be rejected over a two-second discrepancy.
- **Category mismatch.** A run with a skip in it belongs in Any%, not Glitchless. Submitting to the wrong board wastes a good run.

Community reports suggest that rejected submissions are more often the result of sloppy proof than of actual cheating. Record the whole attempt, keep the timer visible, and never trim the video.

## Step-by-Step: Building a Record-Ready Run

The players who hold fast times rarely sit down and "just go fast." They build the run in stages, and each stage has a single job.

| Phase | Focus | Rough time investment | Goal before moving on |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Learn the object | Recognize the needle at any distance or angle | 1–2 hours | You never misidentify a needle |
| 2. Learn the space | Memorize haystack shape, depth, and visual landmarks | 2–4 hours | You can sweep without backtracking |
| 3. Build a route | Decide a fixed search order you repeat every run | 1–2 hours | Same pattern, every attempt |
| 4. Split and measure | Track time at fixed checkpoints | Ongoing | You know where you lose seconds |
| 5. Push for records | Reset aggressively on bad luck | Ongoing | Personal bests become routine |

**The single biggest beginner mistake is a wandering search.** If your eyes jump around the pile randomly, you will re-check the same straw repeatedly and miss obvious sections. Pick a route — front to back, top to bottom, left to right — and never deviate. Boring routes win.

**Second mistake: no splits.** Without checkpoints you cannot tell whether a slow run was bad luck or bad execution. Set two or three split points at natural visual landmarks, record them for twenty runs, and look at the averages. The data will tell you exactly which segment to practice.

**Third mistake: over-resetting.** If you abandon every attempt in the first ten seconds because the haystack "looks wrong," you never practice the late-game sweep, which is where most time is actually lost.

## Route and RNG: Where A Game About Needle In A Haystack World Record Runs Are Won

Every record in a search-based game is a negotiation between skill and luck. You cannot control where the needle spawns, but you can control how much a bad spawn costs you.

| Factor | Controllable? | Impact on final time | How to manage it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Search pattern | Yes | High | Lock a route and repeat it exactly |
| Camera movement speed | Yes | Medium | Smooth, consistent sweeps — no jitter |
| Visual scanning technique | Yes | High | Scan in bands, not in circles |
| Needle spawn location | No | Very high | Reset early if your route makes it unwinnable |
| Distraction / fatigue | Partly | Medium | Short sessions, fixed warm-up |
| Input setup | Yes | Low–medium | Sensitivity you can reproduce every session |

A practical rule: define an early "abort window." If your route reaches a certain checkpoint and you have not found the needle, and historical data says that combination almost never produces a top time, reset. This is not giving up — it is treating attempts as a resource.

Also, warm up. Five minutes of unrecorded runs before a session will do more for your consistency than an hour of extra grinding on tired eyes. Players who track their splits often report that their best times cluster early in a session, before fatigue flattens their scanning speed.

## Submitting Your Run and Getting It Verified

Verification is where good runs go to die. Treat it as part of the run, not an afterthought.

| Checklist item | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Full, uncut recording | Trimming looks like hiding something |
| Timer visible on screen | Reviewers must be able to confirm the time |
| Game audio included | Helps confirm no external assistance |
| Correct category selected | Wrong board = instant rejection |
| Notes on any unusual behavior | Pre-empts reviewer questions |
| Submission to the active leaderboard | Old boards may be locked or archived |

If you are chasing a A Game About Needle In A Haystack world record seriously, keep a local folder of every personal best with its date, category, and splits. When the leaderboard rules change — and for small games they often do — you will already have the proof you need to resubmit.

Finally, be patient with reviewers. Leaderboard moderation is volunteer work, and a polite, well-documented submission moves through the queue far faster than a bare video link with no context.
