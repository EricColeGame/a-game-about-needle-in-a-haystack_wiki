## The Core Loop: What You Are Actually Automating

Before you optimize anything, separate the work into its component parts. Most players treat "searching" as one activity, but it is really four: defining the target, dividing the space, executing checks, and recording results. Automation only works when each part is handled the same way every time.

| Manual behavior | Automatable replacement | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Wandering the field and hoping | A fixed sweep pattern (left-to-right, spiral, quadrant) | Eliminates duplicate checks |
| Remembering where you looked | A checklist or written log | Removes memory as a failure point |
| Re-deciding the order each run | A saved priority list | Saves decision time every session |
| Scanning everything at full attention | Tiered scan: fast pass, then slow pass | Front-loads the highest-probability areas |
| Reacting to misses with a new plan | A pre-written fallback rule | Prevents tilt-driven searching |

The pattern behind all five rows is the same: you are converting judgment calls into rules. Rules can be repeated at speed. Judgment cannot.

Community reports consistently describe the same turning point — players who write down their sweep order finish runs faster than players who "just look carefully." That is not a skill difference. It is a process difference.

## Building Your First Automation Routine

You do not need external tools to get most of the benefit of A Game About Needle In A Haystack automation. You need a routine short enough to remember and rigid enough to trust. The four-step framework below is a good starting point; adjust the numbers to fit how you actually play.

| Step | Action | Time to set up | Payoff |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Define the target | Write one sentence describing exactly what counts as a find | 1 minute | Stops scope creep mid-run |
| 2. Partition the space | Split the field into named zones (A1–C3, or quadrants) | 2–3 minutes | Makes progress measurable |
| 3. Set the sweep order | Rank zones by likelihood and commit to that order | 2 minutes | Removes mid-run hesitation |
| 4. Log as you go | Mark each zone as checked, cleared, or skipped | Ongoing | Prevents repeat work |

Once those four steps are habitual, the routine runs itself. You stop asking "where should I look next?" and start executing. That mental quiet is the actual product of automation — not speed, but the absence of decisions.

### Make the Routine Survive Contact With Reality

A routine that only works when you are fresh is not a routine. Build in two escape hatches: a rule for when you get stuck (for example, "after two full sweeps, switch to the slow pass") and a rule for when you find the target early (log the zone and stop). Players who skip the second rule tend to keep searching out of habit and burn time they did not need to spend.

## Common Automation Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

- **Automating before you understand the target.** A fast routine pointed at the wrong thing is just faster failure. Do one manual run first.
- **Over-engineering the system.** If your tracking document takes longer to maintain than the search takes to complete, simplify it.
- **Changing the sweep order mid-run.** The order is the automation. Changing it converts a system back into guesswork.
- **Trusting memory over the log.** If it is not written down, treat it as unchecked.
- **Ignoring fatigue.** Attention degrades quietly. Short sessions with a hard stop outperform long, unfocused ones.
- **Chasing shortcuts.** Community reports of "instant" solutions rarely hold up; structured searching does.

The through-line is discipline over cleverness. A Game About Needle In A Haystack automation rewards the player who follows an ordinary plan completely far more than the player who improvises brilliantly.
