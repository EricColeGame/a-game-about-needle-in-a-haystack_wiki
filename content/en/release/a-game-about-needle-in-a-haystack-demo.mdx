## What the Search Loop Feels Like in Practice

Based on how the game is presented, the loop is deliberately simple. You survey a haystack, disturb the straw, and look for anomalies — a straight line where everything else is curved, a shine where everything else is matte. Then you grab the needle, the stack resets or expands, and you go again.

What makes or breaks this kind of design is readability. A haystack is, by definition, visual noise: thousands of near-identical yellow strands. The game has to give your eye something to lock onto. In a well-tuned version of this genre, that means subtle lighting differences, slight color variation on the needle, or an audio cue when you get close. The demo is the right place to judge whether those signals feel fair or frustrating.

- **Scanning:** slow, deliberate mouse movement across the pile
- **Revealing:** pushing straw aside to expose deeper layers
- **Spotting:** catching a glint, an edge, or a straight line
- **Confirming:** clicking or grabbing the needle to bank progress
- **Escalating:** a larger or denser haystack on the next round

The A Game About Needle In A Haystack demo is also a useful test of patience. If you find yourself enjoying the near-miss moments — the false alarm, the second pass where you finally see it — the full game will likely land. If the searching feels like a chore after five minutes, you have learned something valuable for free.

## Tips to Find Needles Faster

Whether you are grinding through the demo or preparing for launch, a handful of habits will make you noticeably quicker. These are general search-game principles that apply here directly.

| Tip | Why It Works |
|---|---|
| Sweep in a grid, not at random | Prevents re-checking the same patch twice |
| Drop mouse sensitivity | Small, controlled movements reveal detail |
| Brighten the scene slightly | Metallic glints read better against warm straw |
| Look for straight lines | Straw curves; needles do not |
| Check edges and shadows first | Objects hide well against dark borders |
| Use audio, not just vision | Cues often trigger before the visual appears |
| Take short breaks | Eye fatigue kills detection after about 20 minutes |

The grid sweep is the single biggest upgrade. Pick a corner, move in a slow horizontal line, drop down a row, and reverse. It feels mechanical at first, then becomes automatic — and it stops the classic mistake of scanning the same middle region over and over while the needle sits near the frame edge.

Also resist the urge to click everything. In most versions of this design, false positives cost you time and attention. Wait for the glint or the geometry to confirm before you commit.
